What Information Does a Manufacturer Need to Quote a Custom Part?

When you are ready to request a quote for a custom part, you may already have a technical drawing but be unsure what other information the manufacturer needs.

For many drawing-based components, the useful starting point is simple: Technical Drawing + Required Quantity. The drawing defines what the part needs to be. Quantity provides an important project condition for evaluating how it may be manufactured and quoted.

Complete Drawing ≠ Complete Quotation Context.

The goal is not to provide as much information as possible. It is to provide the information needed to evaluate the specific part and project situation without unnecessary assumptions.

What Information Is Usually Needed?

For a straightforward custom part, the starting requirements are generally standard.

01 · Technical Drawing

The Primary Manufacturing Reference

For a drawing-based custom part, the technical drawing establishes what the finished component is required to be. It may define:

  • dimensions and tolerances;
  • material;
  • threads and other features;
  • surface finish;
  • heat treatment or coating; and
  • inspection or other technical requirements.

A 3D model can sometimes help communicate geometry, but it does not necessarily replace a drawing when tolerances, material, treatment or other manufacturing requirements need to be defined.

02 · Required Quantity

The Volume Context

Quantity can affect how a custom part is evaluated for manufacturing. Prototype, small-batch and repeat-production quantities may not fit the same manufacturing approach or cost structure equally well.

Providing the actual required quantity therefore helps the manufacturer evaluate the drawing under the volume conditions the project actually requires. If future repeat quantities are already known and relevant, they can also be useful.

When Is Drawing + Quantity Not Enough?

For a straightforward custom part, Drawing + Quantity may be enough to begin quotation. Additional information becomes useful when there is a specific manufacturing or sourcing situation behind the RFQ.

If MOQ Is the Problem Useful evidence: Drawing + Required Quantity + Supplier MOQ + Supplier Feedback
This provides context for understanding what may be contributing to the current minimum quantity.
If a Supplier Is Asking for a Drawing Change Useful evidence: Drawing + Proposed Change + Supplier Reason
This allows the proposed change to be reviewed together with the manufacturing reason behind it rather than looking at the drawing change in isolation.
If a Prototype Is Difficult to Repeat Useful evidence: Drawing + Sample Result + Repeat-Production Problem
A successful sample shows what was achieved under the sample conditions. The repeat-production problem shows what is now proving difficult to reproduce consistently.
If Different Suppliers Are Responding Differently Useful evidence: Drawing + Relevant Supplier Responses
Different quotations, questions, MOQ requirements or manufacturing feedback can provide useful context for understanding how different suppliers are evaluating the same drawing.
The Core Principle Additional information is useful when it helps answer the specific manufacturing or quotation question behind the RFQ.

What Other Information Can Affect a Quote?

Depending on the part and project, other information may also matter.

Material and Treatment Requirements

If material, heat treatment, plating or coating requirements are already clearly defined on the drawing, they do not need to be repeated separately. If they are still open or unclear, that should be identified rather than left for the manufacturer to assume.

Critical Functional Requirements

Sometimes a feature is particularly important to assembly, positioning, movement, wear or another equipment function, but that importance is not obvious from the drawing alone. Brief application context can be useful when it changes how the manufacturer should evaluate or verify that requirement. This does not mean the complete machine or application needs to be explained.

Relevant Manufacturing History

Previous supplier feedback, inspection results, production problems or sample results can be useful when they directly relate to the current manufacturing question. Information that does not affect the manufacturing or quotation judgment does not need to be added simply to make the RFQ more complete.

What Manufacturing Question Needs to Be Answered?

The manufacturer needs enough information to answer:

What information is needed to evaluate this drawing at the required quantity without making assumptions that could affect the quotation or manufacturing judgment?

For a straightforward part, the drawing and quantity may provide most of what is needed. For a part already experiencing a sourcing, manufacturing or production issue, relevant project evidence may also be necessary.

This does not mean the drawing itself is incomplete. The drawing defines the required part. The quotation context includes the project conditions that can materially affect how that part should be evaluated and quoted.

What Should You Send First?

For most drawing-based custom parts, start with:

Technical Drawing + Required Quantity

Then add only the information relevant to the current situation. That might include:

  • supplier manufacturing feedback;
  • a proposed drawing change;
  • supplier MOQ;
  • sample or inspection results;
  • repeat-production problems; or
  • other project information that materially affects the manufacturing or quotation judgment.

You do not need to prepare a complete manufacturing analysis before contacting a manufacturer. The initial information only needs to be sufficient to determine: What can be evaluated now, and what—if anything—still needs clarification before quotation or manufacturing review?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a technical drawing enough to get a custom part quoted?

Sometimes. For a straightforward drawing-based component, the drawing and required quantity may be enough to begin quotation. Additional information becomes useful when something outside the drawing can materially affect the manufacturing or quotation judgment.

Do I need a 3D model to request a quote?

Not necessarily. A 3D model can help communicate geometry, but a technical drawing is often still needed when tolerances, material, treatment or other manufacturing requirements must be defined.

Why does a manufacturer need the quantity before quoting?

Quantity can affect manufacturing approach, setup economics, material purchasing and production planning. The same drawing may therefore be evaluated differently at prototype, small-batch and repeat-production quantities.

Should I send previous supplier feedback?

When it is relevant to the current manufacturing or quotation question, yes. Feedback about MOQ, manufacturing difficulty, proposed drawing changes, inspection results or repeat-production problems can provide useful context that the drawing alone may not show.
